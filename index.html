Concept case study for Instacart

Making grocery swaps feel less like a gamble

What if you could tell your grocery app how your household works, once, and have every future order respect it?

My role
Product manager, solo concept project
Timeline
3 weeks
What I worked on
Discovery, problem framing, PRD, metrics, experiment design
Status
Proposal with a validation plan

The short version

One problem, one bet, one way to know if it worked

The problem

Replacement preferences are set item by item, order by order. That’s tedious, so most people skip it and let the shopper guess.

What I did

Mapped today’s flow, pulled together public research on substitution behavior, and compared three ways to fix it.

The proposal

Household Rules: standing preferences like “unsweetened only” that apply to every future order and show up for the shopper right when they need them.

How I’d measure it

More orders where every swap is accepted, without slowing shoppers down or pushing refunds up.

Understanding the problem

Every grocery order has a few surprises baked in

If you’ve ever ordered groceries online, you know the moment. Your phone buzzes in the middle of a meeting: the oat milk is out, and your shopper wants to know if vanilla is okay. Sometimes you catch it. Often you don’t, and the vanilla shows up at your door.

Stockouts are a normal part of grocery retail. Shelves empty faster than they’re restocked, especially on weekends. So the question isn’t how to make every item available. It’s how to make sure that when something is missing, the customer still gets what they would have chosen.

Instacart has clearly invested here already. Before I proposed anything, I wanted to understand what exists today and where it still falls short.

How replacements work today

For each item in the cart, a customer can choose one of three options:

The default

Find best match

The shopper picks a similar item, guided by Instacart’s replacement suggestions.

Pick specific replacement

The customer chooses a backup product ahead of time.

Don’t replace

The item is refunded if it’s unavailable.

On top of that, Instacart asks for alternatives on items that look likely to be low in stock, uses a machine learning model to rank replacement options, and lets the shopper message the customer to approve swaps in real time. That’s a strong foundation. But three things still break.

  • The work is per item, which is too much

    Asking people to make a replacement decision for each of 20 to 40 items is a lot. Baymard Institute’s research on grocery sites found that this kind of item-by-item setup quickly becomes unwieldy, and that people need easier ways to express what they want.

  • What you tell the app doesn’t seem to stick

    A choice made for this Sunday’s oat milk doesn’t turn into a lasting rule about how your household buys milk. Next week, you’re back to the default.

  • The safety net assumes you’re free

    Real-time chat works if you’re watching your phone. If you’re at work, driving, or putting a kid to bed, the shopper has to guess, and the guess is final.

People don’t have opinions about forty items. They have a handful of rules about how their household shops.

This became my working hypothesis. A parent doesn’t care which brand of paper towels shows up, but they will absolutely notice the wrong cereal. If the app could learn those few rules, it would stop asking for decisions people don’t want to make, and stop guessing on the ones they care about.

Who this is for

Two customers and one person in the middle

These are proto-personas, built from public research and my own assumptions. They’re a starting point for interviews, not a replacement for them.

P

Priya, the weekly restocker

Two kids, orders from her phone at lunch

Her list barely changes week to week. She cares deeply about four or five items and not at all about the rest. She doesn’t have time to set preferences on every line.

“Any paper towels are fine. Just not the wrong cereal.”
M

Marcus, the particular one

Shops organic, reads every label

He would rather get a refund than a swap that breaks his standards. A non-organic replacement isn’t a small miss for him. It’s a reason to stop using the app.

“If it’s not organic, don’t bother. Just refund me.”
S

The shopper

Secondary user, often juggling several orders

They want clear instructions, fewer back-and-forth messages, and no extra screens. Any solution that slows them down in the aisle fails, no matter how much customers like it.

“Tell me what they want and let me move on.”

Being honest about what I don’t know

The assumptions this idea rests on

Before writing a single requirement, I listed what has to be true for this to work. The riskiest ones are what I’d test first.

The second assumption matters most. If people’s preferences really are item-specific, Household Rules turns into per-item settings with extra steps, and the whole idea falls apart.

Exploring solutions

Three ways to fix it, and why I picked the hardest one

How might we let customers express what they care about once, so swaps match their intent without adding work for them or the shopper?

OptionWhat it isStrengthWeaknessEffort
A. One global settingA single switch: always replace or never replaceFast to build, easy to understandToo blunt. Priya wants swaps on paper towels and refunds on cereal.Small
B. Smarter defaults onlyKeep improving the model; no new customer controlsInvisible, no new UI to learnCan’t capture stated values like “we only buy organic.” Wrong guesses still erode trust.Medium
C. Household RulesStanding, category-level rules that apply to every orderCaptures intent once. Works with the model: rules set the limits, the model ranks within them.More scope, and rules can conflict with each otherLarge

Option C costs more, but it’s the only one that solves both halves of the problem: less work for customers and better outcomes on the items they care about. It also makes the existing model more valuable instead of competing with it.

The solution

Household Rules, in three moments

The key design decision was when to ask. Nobody wants a setup wizard for their groceries. So instead of asking up front, the app asks right after a swap goes wrong, when the preference is fresh and the customer is motivated to fix it.

Four kinds of rules cover most of what people care about

Brand

“This brand only” or “any brand is fine.”

Attribute

“Organic only,” “unsweetened,” “same size or bigger.”

Price

“No more than $1 over the original.”

Fallback

“If nothing fits, refund me.”

Prioritization

What ships first, and what waits

I cut scope by asking one question about every feature: does the first version fail without it? If not, it waits.

In the first version

  • Save a rule from a rejected swap, in one tap
  • The four rule types above
  • Rule tags on cart items
  • Rules shown to shoppers, with breaking options greyed out
  • A simple page to edit or delete rules

Later, once the core works

  • Suggested rules based on past orders
  • Shared rules for everyone in a household
  • Rules that differ by store
  • A monthly “your rules saved you 4 bad swaps” recap

Measuring success

How I’d know it’s working

North Star metric

Orders delivered as expected

The share of orders where every swap was accepted and no refund happened that the customer didn’t ask for. It captures what customers actually feel: “I got what I meant to order.”

Signals that it’s being used

  • Share of active customers with at least one rule
  • Share of cart items covered by a rule
  • Save rate on the “Want this to stick?” prompt
  • Swap acceptance on rule-covered items vs. uncovered ones

Guardrails I wouldn’t let slip

  • Shopper time per out-of-stock item
  • Refund rate, since strict rules could shrink baskets
  • Order cancellations
  • Support contacts about replacements

The experiment

Hypothesis
If customers can save standing rules, orders delivered as expected will go up, because shoppers will make fewer guesses on the items customers care about.
Setup
A/B test randomized by customer, not by order, so the same household doesn’t see both versions. Start in one or two metro areas.
How long
At least four to six weeks. Most people order weekly, and a rule only proves its value on the second or third order after it’s saved.
Ship if
The North Star improves meaningfully and no guardrail gets worse. If refunds rise, I’d look at which rules are causing it before deciding anything.

Roadmap

From pilot to default experience

Phase 1, months 1 to 3

Prove the core

Ship the first version to a test group in a few markets. Validate that rules get saved and that shoppers can follow them without slowing down.

Phase 2, months 4 to 6

Make it effortless

Suggest rules automatically from order history, so customers get value before anything goes wrong.

Phase 3, months 7 and on

Make it shared

Let everyone in a household see and edit the same rules, and adapt rules to each store’s catalog.

Looking back

What I learned

The hard part wasn’t the screens

I expected to spend most of my time on the interface. Instead, the hardest questions were about definitions. What counts as a “good” swap? Who decides when two rules disagree? Getting those answers right mattered more than any button.

What I’d do with more time

I’d talk to shoppers first. This idea lives or dies in the aisle, not in the app. If rules add even a few seconds per item across thousands of orders, that’s a real cost, and I’d want to see it before building anything.
