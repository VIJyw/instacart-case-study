🔍 Understanding the problem

Every grocery order has a few surprises baked in

If you’ve ever ordered groceries online, you know the moment. Your phone buzzes in the middle of a meeting: the oat milk is out, and your shopper wants to know if vanilla is okay. Sometimes you catch it. Often you don’t, and the vanilla shows up at your door.

Stockouts are a normal part of grocery retail, so the goal isn’t to make every item available. It’s to make sure that when something is missing, the customer still gets what they would have chosen.

How replacements work today

🤝

Find best match Default

The shopper picks a similar item, guided by Instacart’s replacement suggestions.

👆

Pick specific replacement

The customer chooses a backup product ahead of time.

💸

Don’t replace

The item is refunded if it’s unavailable.

Instacart already prompts for alternatives on likely low-stock items, ranks replacements with a machine learning model, and lets shoppers message customers. It’s a strong foundation, but three things still break.

🥱

Too much work per item

A 25-item cart means up to 25 decisions. Baymard Institute found item-by-item substitution setup quickly becomes unwieldy.

🔁

Preferences don’t stick

A choice for this Sunday’s oat milk doesn’t become a lasting rule about how your household buys milk.

📵

The safety net needs you free

Real-time chat only works if you’re watching your phone. Otherwise the shopper guesses, and the guess is final.

People don’t have opinions about 40 items. They have a handful of rules about how their household shops.

This became my core hypothesis. A parent doesn’t care which paper towels show up, but they will absolutely notice the wrong cereal.

📈 Measuring success

How I’d know it’s working

⭐ North Star metric

Orders delivered as expected

The share of orders where every swap was accepted and no refund happened that the customer didn’t choose. It measures what customers actually feel: “I got what I meant to order.”

Modeled baseline~78%
Target~86%

📍 Input metrics and targets

  • Save rate on the “Want this to stick?” prompt: 25%+
  • Active customers with at least one rule: 15% by week 6
  • Cart items covered by a rule: 10%+
  • Swap acceptance, covered vs. uncovered items: +10 pts

🚧 Guardrails with hard limits

  • Shopper time per out-of-stock item: +5 sec max
  • Refund rate: +0.5 pts max
  • Order cancellations: no significant increase
  • Support contacts about replacements: flat or down

🧪 The experiment

Hypothesis
If customers can save standing rules, orders delivered as expected will rise by at least 2 points, because shoppers make fewer guesses on items customers care about.
Design
A/B test randomized by customer, not by order, so one household never sees both versions. Start in 2 metro areas.
Sample size
~6,800 customers per arm to detect a 2-point change from a 78% baseline (95% confidence, 80% power). I’d plan for well above that, since orders from the same customer aren’t independent.
Duration
6 weeks. Most people order weekly, and a rule proves its value on the 2nd or 3rd order after it’s saved.
Decision rule
Ship if the North Star improves by 2+ points and every guardrail holds. If refunds rise, find the rules causing it before deciding.

🗺️ Roadmap

From pilot to default, with a gate at every step

Phase 1, months 1–3

🌱 Prove the core

Ship v1 to 5% of customers in 2 metros.

Gate: 25%+ prompt save rate, guardrails hold
Phase 2, months 4–6

🤖 Make it effortless

Suggest rules from order history; expand to 50% of customers.

Gate: +2 pts North Star, statistically significant
Phase 3, months 7+

👨‍👩‍👧 Make it shared

Household-wide rules and store-specific catalogs; roll out to 100%.

Gate: refund rate within +0.5 pts

🪞 Looking back

What I learned

The hard part wasn’t the screens

The hardest questions were about definitions. What counts as a “good” swap? Who wins when two rules disagree? Getting those right mattered more than any button.

What I’d do next

Talk to shoppers first. This idea lives or dies in the aisle, not in the app. Even 5 extra seconds per item across millions of orders is a real cost.
